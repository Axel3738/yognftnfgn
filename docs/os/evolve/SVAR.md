## Efterköpsenkäten (frågan 2026-09-30)

Svaret kom 2026-09-30 kväll. Axel klistrade in det. Det står här ordagrant,
bara med `>` framför varje rad. Källorna `[C1]`–`[C6]` är trådar i
community-chatten och `[D1]` är ett dokument. Boten citerar inte samtalen från
kursen. Se "Där kursens egna citat säger något annat" nedan.

> The community runs thank-you page surveys and review mining as their primary VoC methods, but no source ranks all five methods by winners or measures hit rate of survey-sourced concepts against other sources. Here's what's grounded and what isn't.
>
> 1. Methods — ranked by what members actually run
>
> No source ranks these. What we can confirm members run:
> * Thank-you page surveys: Sem runs them directly on the thank-you page, not a separate survey page [C1]. Spencer covers a customer feedback survey framework in the CRO program [C4]. Most-discussed method in the community.
> * Review mining (Amazon + social): D1 shows this producing a $200K+ winning comparison ad — Shulex to scrape Amazon reviews (pros, cons, usage scenarios), plus TikTok/YouTube comments surfaced pain points like mold and constant filter changes that became the winning angle [D1].
> * Post-delivery surveys: Chrisphillips tried sending a second survey ~2 weeks after delivery for product-improvement feedback — "they never did that survey" [C3]. Zero response.
> * Giveaway surveys: Discussed as an incentive mechanism [C6], not as a standalone VoC method.
> * Interviews: Not covered in any source.
>
> The insight: thank-you page (peak buying momentum) and review mining (existing public feedback) are the two methods with traction. Post-delivery surveys fail because the moment has passed.
>
> 2. Questions — open vs multiple choice, timing
> * Open/written answers over multiple choice: Spencer is explicit — "I usually like written answers, because then people actually have to type shit, and you can see the people who just filled it out for shits versus the people who actually took time to write shit out" [C4]. Multiple choice lets low-effort respondents skate through.
> * Purpose is to surface the non-obvious: Karos's question references Spencer's example — they thought they were selling to pregnant women, but surveys revealed overweight men with beer bellies, which unlocked new scale [C5]. The survey exists to find what your ads don't already say.
> * Thank-you page, not after delivery: Sem confirms thank-you page placement [C1]. Chrisphillips's post-delivery survey got zero responses [C3] — reinforcing that the thank-you page is where you capture answers while buying intent is highest.
> * No exact question wording appears in any source. Spencer references his CRO program's survey framework [C4] but the specific questions aren't in these conversations.
>
> 3. Setup — response rates, incentives, languages, gifts, scope
>
> Mostly uncovered:
> * Response rates per channel: No source gives numbers. Chrisphillips's post-delivery survey got zero responses [C3] — the only data point.
> * Incentive design: Spencer prefers sweepstakes over per-response incentives — "why not do a sweepstake and save money / make money" [C6]. If it's a long survey, incentivizing every response makes sense; otherwise "just no need IMO" [C6]. No source discusses incentive bias on answer quality.
> * Languages (12 markets): Not covered.
> * Gift-buyer (buyer ≠ user): Not covered.
> * One survey for all stores vs per-product: Not covered. Spencer does say survey data should be used to tailor Klaviyo campaigns — "make that like the first email they get" or "based on post purchase survey data" [C2] — which implies per-niche tailoring, but he doesn't specify survey structure.
> * Tool: Amitoj uses Triple Whale for post-purchase surveys [C4].
>
> 4. Into the creative funnel — tagging, routing, validation
> * Tying answers to the driving ad: Spencer says this isn't reliable — "UTMs are never accurate. Not really sure it's needed to go this granular tbh" [C2]. Don't try to attribute survey answers to specific ads.
> * Downstream use: Survey data feeds into the first email and Klaviyo campaign segmentation [C2], not directly into creative briefs in any documented workflow.
> * Tagging framework (desires/objections/avatars → cold hooks vs objection ads): No source covers a tagging system for survey answers. D1 shows a research-to-ad pipeline (research → desires/awareness stage → ad concept → document in Excel) but from review mining, not survey data [D1].
> * How many answers to trust a pattern: Not covered.
> * Testing outliers without chasing noise: Not covered.
> * New angle vs iteration: D1's iteration framework tests hook, headline, benefits, and persona variations on a winning concept [D1] — but this is ad iteration mechanics, not survey-to-creative routing.
>
> 5. Proof — measured hit rate of survey-sourced vs other concepts
>
> No source covers this. Nobody has shared data comparing hit rate of survey-sourced ad concepts against comment mining, competitor swipes, or iterations. D1 shows review mining producing a winner [D1], and C5 references survey data unlocking a new avatar [C5], but neither provides a comparative hit-rate measurement.
>
> Bottom line: The strongest guidance is Spencer's preference for open-ended questions on the thank-you page [C1][C4], the principle that surveys should surface non-obvious avatars [C5], and review mining as a proven method via D1's $200K ad example. Response rates, incentive bias, multi-language setup, gift-buyer considerations, tagging frameworks, validation thresholds, and comparative hit-rate evidence are all gaps — worth asking the community directly, especially other operators running multi-niche stores.
