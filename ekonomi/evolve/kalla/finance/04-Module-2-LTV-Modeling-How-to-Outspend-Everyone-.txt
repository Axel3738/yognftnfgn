1) What this lesson covers

This video answers one question:

How do I spend more than every competitor… and still make money?

That’s the real reason LTV exists. Not because it’s a “nice metric.”
 Because it tells you how hard you can press the gas without dying.

Your goal (and mine) is simple:

Spend as much as humanly possible

while generating positive contribution margin

and still leaving profit at the business level

2) The purpose of LTV (in this course)
LTV is a weapon

In our context, LTV exists to answer:

“How can I outspend everyone else in the marketplace while still making a profit?”

Because if you can spend more than everyone else, you win distribution.
 And if you win distribution, you win the market.

3) Where the data comes from (and where it doesn’t)
Pull customer metrics from Shopify, not Facebook

You can use Meta/Google for spend, but you should measure new customers from Shopify.

Why? Because ad platforms will happily lie to your face with attribution.

4) CAC vs CPA (stop mixing these)
CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost)

CAC in this module means:

CAC = Total ad spend in a time period / Net new customers acquired in that same time period

And “net new customers” means Shopify-filtered:

New customer = TRUE

Not returning customers

Not blended “orders”

Example:

Spend $1,000,000

Acquire 1,000,000 new customers

CAC = $1

CPA (Cost per Purchase)

CPA is cost per purchase/event.
 CAC is cost per new customer.

If you mix those, your model is fake.

5) LTV vs retention (also not the same thing)

Retention is people staying.
 LTV is dollars they generate.

You can have:

decent retention but low dollars (small orders)

mediocre retention but huge dollars (high AOV + upsells)

That’s why we model LTV in dollars, not “customer counts.”

Recommended tool: Lifetimely

You can do it other ways, but Lifetimely is:

cheap

accurate

easy for cohort LTV by month

Use whatever you want as long as you get correct cohort LTV.




6) The spreadsheet: what it’s doing (so you know how to use it)

This template is the backbone for the whole module.

The big concept: cohort math

A cohort = customers acquired in the same period (ex: February).

We track what that February cohort does over time:

Month 0 (purchase month)

Month 1

Month 2

etc.

Column A: “Post this month cohort retention (LTV %)”

Month 0 is 100% by definition (baseline).

You’ll often see Month 0 at 101–102% in real data because some customers reorder inside the same 28-day window. Don’t overthink it—just use the data.

This column is an LTV multiplier.
 If Month 1 is 60%, that means:

If Month 0 = $1, Month 1 adds $0.60 more.
 If Month 0 = $100, Month 1 adds $60 more.

Column B: Revenue

Revenue is just:
 LTV % × AOV

So Month 1 revenue is:
 Month 1 LTV % × AOV

Column C/D: Margin and CAC interaction (the “truth” column)

This is where the model stops being cute and starts being useful.

You take:

revenue

multiply by your contribution margin %

then subtract CAC (first order only)

This shows you:

are we negative on Month 0?

when do we cross into profit?

how long is payback?

Total revenue (cohort) and Total margin (cohort)

Total revenue shows cumulative dollars collected from that cohort over time.

Total margin shows cumulative contribution profit from that cohort over time.

Total margin is the scaling decision.
 If total margin is positive fast enough, you can scale.
 If it isn’t, you’re buying revenue and renting dopamine.

8) Why first order and repeat order are split (this is huge)

Your margin is often different on:

first order

repeat orders

Because first orders can include:

free gifts

special intro bundles

higher discounting

one-click upsells that don’t repeat

Classic example: razor companies

first shipment includes the razor handle (costly)

repeats are just blades (higher margin)

So the model lets you input:

AOV + margin on first order

AOV + margin on repeat orders



This is how you stop lying to yourself.

9) OpEx (the “don’t worry yet, but you’ll thank me later” section)

OpEx = operating expenses (payroll, rent, software, etc.)

You generally don’t need it for early modeling, but once you scale, OpEx matters because it tells you how much overhead gets absorbed by LTV.

The template uses a simple approach:

Estimate monthly revenue

Estimate transactions (revenue ÷ AOV)

Divide OpEx across transactions

Convert to OpEx-per-order or OpEx as % of revenue

Example from your transcript:

OpEx = $100,000

Revenue target = $10,000,000

AOV = $64

Transactions ≈ 10,000,000 / 64 = 156,250

OpEx per transaction ≈ 100,000 / 156,250 = $0.64

Which is about ~1%



That’s how your “true” cost stack evolves:

COGS % might be 20%

COGS + OpEx might become 21.6% depending on inputs

Again: not required at first. Powerful later.

11) Common mistakes

Pulling CAC from Facebook instead of Shopify new customers

Using CPA and calling it CAC

Modeling “customers” instead of “dollars”

Ignoring that first order margin ≠ repeat margin

Treating upsells as recurring when they’re usually one-time

Overcomplicating OpEx too early and never shipping decisions

12) Quiz (with answers)

What’s the purpose of LTV in this course?
 Answer: To determine how much you can outspend competitors while still making contribution margin and profit.

How do you calculate CAC the way we’re using it here?
 Answer: Total spend in a time period ÷ net new customers from Shopify in that same period.

Why not pull “new customers” from Facebook?
 Answer: Attribution distortion—Shopify is the source of truth for new vs returning.

Why is LTV not the same as retention?
 Answer: Retention is about people staying; LTV is about dollars generated over time.

Why split first order and repeat order AOV/margins?
Answer: The cost stack and revenue stack often change after the first order (free gifts, intro bundles, one-time upsells, etc.)
